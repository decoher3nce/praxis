# Coach Guide: The Operating Manual

This is the manual for running Praxis. It walks each phase of the seven-step loop and gives you actual things to *say* and *ask*, not just principles. Read it once end to end; return to the relevant section before each session.

A word before the mechanics. The engine of this work is the **Socratic method**: you lead not by handing over answers but by asking the questions that draw the student's own reasoning out. Socrates called himself a midwife of ideas. He produced nothing himself; he helped others give birth to what they already half-knew. That is your stance. Your job is not to transfer your judgment into the student but to grow *theirs*. The discipline is simple to state and hard to keep: ask more than you tell. When you catch yourself about to deliver the answer, ask the question that lets them find it instead. You will be slower, and they will be more capable. That trade is the entire point.

---

## The flow at a glance

```
        PHASE 1: Observe & nominate               (offline / at manager's request)
        spot gaps AND strengths worth taking to great
                       │
                       ▼
        PHASE 2: Contract (opt-in)                (initial meeting)
        invite · consent · "is now the time?" (bandwidth)
                       │
          decline ◄────┤   no cost, revisit later
                       │ opt in
                       ▼
        PHASE 3: Present a few; student chooses    (same meeting, or a second)
        invitational · evidence · "where will this land?" (relevance)
                       │
       pass cycle ◄────┤   fully legitimate, revisit later
                       │ chooses one
                       ▼
        PHASE 4: Build the path                    (offline prep → present)
        curate a fine-tuned, multimodal strategy, no data-dump
                       │
                       ▼
        PHASE 5: Practice                          (student, between sessions)
        run the activity on real work
                       │
                       ▼
        PHASE 6: Check in & adjust  ◄──────────┐   (recurring meetings)
        debrief · keep / tweak / swap          │
                       │                        │ keep practicing
                       ├────────────────────────┘
                       ▼
        PHASE 7: Reflect & close                   (closing meeting)
        continue · switch dimension · graduate
                       │
      new cycle ◄──────┤
     (back to 1 / 3)   │ graduate
                       ▼
        student coaches someone else  →  more leaders
```

**When each phase happens.** Phase 1 is offline, during your normal interactions with the person, or prompted by a manager's request. Phases 2 and 3 happen in an initial meeting (or split across two). Phase 4 is your offline preparation, followed by presenting the curated material to the student. Phase 5 is the student practicing on real work between sessions. Phase 6 is the recurring check-in. Phase 7 is a closing conversation that either starts a new cycle or graduates them.

---

## Phase 1: Observe & nominate

**What you're doing.** Before any conversation, you identify a few dimensions worth developing for this person. You're looking for *observable* signal (things you could actually cite), not personality verdicts.

**Look for strengths, not just gaps.** The instinct is to hunt for weaknesses and shore them up. Don't let that be the whole game. A leader who is merely *good* at every dimension is often only an average leader; greatness usually comes from a few dimensions taken from good to *great*. So look both ways: for the gap worth closing *and* for the existing strength worth amplifying. Often the highest-leverage move is to take someone from good to exceptional in a dimension they already lean on, not to repair a weakness they'll never love.

**How to do it well.**
- Anchor on evidence you could actually cite: a recent project, a meeting, a piece of feedback, a moment where something went well or got stuck. Recent and concrete beats abstract and aspirational.
- Look for dimensions where development would have somewhere real to land in the next month or two. A dimension with no live work to practice on will stall at Phase 5.
- Hold them loosely. They are nominations, not a diagnosis. The student may pick none of them and name something truer.

**Entry-point flexibility.** This phase has three valid starting points: you nominate; the student brings their own dimension; or you both start from a recent **review**, a formal performance review or just informal feedback. All three are legitimate. If the student already knows what they want to work on, your nominations become a check ("here's what I was seeing, does that match?") rather than a menu.

## Phase 2: Contracting

**What you're doing.** Opening the engagement with an explicit, opt-in agreement. The output is a shared understanding. Walk through the expectations handout together (`02-coaching-expectations.md`). The real output is trust.

**The hardest part: making the invitation land as a gift, not a flag.** People in a performance culture hear "I'd like to coach you" as "I have found a deficiency." Defuse that directly.

Things to say:
- *"This is development, not correction. I'm offering it because I think you're worth investing in, not because something's wrong."*
- *"This is opt-in, genuinely. You can say no to the whole thing right now and it costs you nothing. You can also start and stop later. I'd rather you opt in for real than go along with it."*

**A light note on the dual role.** If you're also this person's manager, acknowledge it once, plainly, and move on. It isn't a crisis, just an honesty: most of what you discuss stays between you, and you'll flag anything that needs to touch the record. The expectations handout carries the detail (`02-coaching-expectations.md`); in conversation, keep it brief so it doesn't loom larger than it is.

**Check bandwidth: is now the right time?** This is half of readiness (the other half, relevance, comes in Phase 3; full detail in `03-readiness-and-timing.md`). Before committing, make sure they actually have room. A development push at the wrong moment is a burden, not a gift.
- *"Honestly, do you have capacity for this right now? What's your work and life load looking like over the next month?"*
- *"If now isn't the time, that's a real answer, not a cop-out. We can pick it up later."*

**Closing the contracting conversation:**
- *"What would make this worth your time? What would you want to be true a few months from now?"*
- *"What's your read on the right cadence, every two weeks or monthly? What's realistic given your load?"*

## Phase 3: Presenting the dimensions

**What you're doing.** Offering the nominated dimensions invitationally, and supporting the student to choose one, or to pass.

**Framing that is invitational and evidence-based.** Lead with what you observed, not with a label. "You're weak at delegation" is a verdict. "I noticed in the last review cycle you were doing a lot of the integration sign-offs yourself, and I'm curious how that felt" is an observation that opens a door.

Things to say:
- *"Here are a few I've been seeing. I'll tell you what's behind each one, then you tell me which, if any, feels live right now."*
- For each: name the dimension, cite the specific evidence, and stop. Don't argue for it. *"That's what I noticed. What's your read?"*

**Check relevance: does it have somewhere to land?** This is the other half of readiness. A dimension worth choosing should connect to something live in the student's current work, so the practice has a real place to happen.
- *"Where in your current work would you actually practice this? Is there something coming up where it matters?"*
- If there's no live landing spot, steer toward a dimension that has one, or agree to revisit this one later.

**Making "I'll pass this cycle" a fully legitimate answer.** This is essential and easy to undermine with tone. Say it plainly and mean it:
- *"A completely valid answer is 'none of these, not now.' If you're underwater or none of these is the real thing, passing is the right call, not a failure. We can revisit anytime."*
- If they pass: *"Good. Thanks for being honest about it. That's exactly the muscle this whole thing runs on."* Then actually let it go. If you punish a pass, you will never get an honest one again.

## Phase 4: Building the path

**What you're doing.** Offline, you assemble the development plan for the chosen dimension (a short set of learning inputs plus a sequence of practice activities) and then present it to the student. Each file in `04-dimensions/` gives you a starting learning path and activity sequence; your job is to *curate and tailor*, not to hand it over as written.

**Guidelines for building a good path:**
- **Curate, don't data-dump.** The failure mode here is dumping a stack of articles and a pile of activities on the student. Resist it. Pick the few inputs and the one or two activities that fit *this* student, *this* dimension, and *this* moment in their work. A fine-tuned strategy beats a comprehensive reading list every time.
- **Go multimodal, lightly.** Offer a mix (something to read, something to watch, something to do or observe) and let the student take in ideas the way that actually works for them. Skip whatever doesn't help.
- **Anchor every activity to real, current work.** Practice has to land on something live: an upcoming meeting, a real decision, an actual deliverable. If an activity has no real place to happen, redesign it until it does.
- **Sequence by the science of learning.** The activity arc in each dimension file is built on a few robust findings; preserve their spirit:
  - *Progressive challenge / deliberate practice*: low-stakes reps before the visible stretch, each rep just past comfortable (Ericsson; see References).
  - *Spacing*: spread practice across check-ins rather than cramming; spaced practice outlasts massed practice.
  - *Retrieval*: have reflection reconstruct what happened from memory rather than reread notes; retrieval is what consolidates learning.
- **Use a coaching frame for the conversation.** A simple structure such as GROW (Goal, Reality, Options, Will) keeps you asking rather than telling. See References.

**What to say when you present it:**
- *"Here's a small, curated path, not a syllabus. A couple of things to take in, and one or two things to actually try on real work."*
- *"Which of these fits how you learn? Skip the rest."*
- *"Where on your actual plate could the practice happen?"*

> **This section is still developing.** Over time, the `04-dimensions/` files will grow a deeper bank of suggested readings, video clips, and references for you to draw from, and this guidance will expand with them: more concrete research, more worked examples of well-built paths. For now the principle holds: curate a tailored strategy, don't assign a stack.

## Phase 5: Practice

**What you're doing.** Stepping back while the student takes action. This phase is theirs: they run the activity on real work, inside the flow of their actual job, not as a separate exercise. Your role recedes to support: available if they get stuck, but not hovering. The rep is where the learning is earned; let them earn it.

---

## Phase 6: Check-ins (the loop in motion)

**What you're doing.** Recurring conversations where you debrief the most recent activity and decide what's next. This is where action and reflection actually cycle.

**How to debrief an activity: use retrieval, not review.** Don't let them reread their notes and report a summary. Make them reconstruct it from memory; reconstruction is where the learning consolidates.
- *"Without looking at anything, walk me through what actually happened. What did you do, and what happened next?"*
- *"Where did it go differently than you expected?"*
- *"What did you notice about yourself in the moment?"*
- *"If you ran it again tomorrow, what's the one thing you'd change?"*

**The decision rule: keep, tweak, or swap.** After each debrief, decide together what to do with the activity:
- **Keep** it (run it again, same form) when it's working and there's more to extract from another rep, or when spacing it out will deepen it.
- **Tweak** it when the activity is right but the difficulty, scope, or framing is off: too safe, too big, wrong context.
- **Swap** it entirely when it's not producing learning, the dimension itself has shifted, or real work has overtaken the plan.

A simple way to choose: *"Did this produce learning or just activity? If learning, keep or push harder. If just activity, what would actually move you?"*

## Hard moments

These are the conversations that separate a real coach from a cheerleader.

**The blind-spot conversation (self rates high, you rate low).** The most delicate quadrant on the 2×2 (`05-reflection-2x2.md`). The instinct to lead with your verdict is exactly wrong: it triggers defense and ends learning.
- Lead with curiosity and specific evidence, never a verdict: *"Can we look at a couple of recent examples together? I want to check my read against yours. I might be missing context."*
- Put the evidence on the table, not the judgment: describe what happened, not what it means about them.
- Let the gap surface in *their* words if you can. *"How do you think that landed for the team?"* gets further than *"that landed badly."*
- It is a win if they leave merely *aware* there's a gap. Awareness precedes skill.

**Resistance.** If the student pushes back on a dimension or the whole engagement:
- Get curious before you get persuasive: *"Tell me more about that. What's the objection?"*
- Resistance is data. Sometimes the dimension is wrong. Sometimes the timing is. Sometimes trust isn't there yet. Diagnose before you push.
- Never override a genuine no. You can revisit later; you cannot un-coerce.

**The student who's overloaded mid-cycle.** Work or life has spiked.
- Name it for them so they don't have to confess it: *"You sound buried. Do we pause this?"*
- Pausing is not quitting. *"Let's park it cleanly and pick up when there's room. The work it's pointing at will still be here."*
- A coach who protects bandwidth earns the right to push later.

**When to pause entirely.** Pause when bandwidth has collapsed, when trust has been damaged and needs repair first, or when a real crisis (a launch, an anomaly, a personal emergency) deserves the student's whole attention. The loop will keep.

## Phase 7: Closing & graduating

**What you're doing.** Closing a cycle: reflect on the dimension (the 2×2 in `05-reflection-2x2.md` is the tool for this), name the movement, and decide what's next: continue, switch dimensions, or graduate the student toward coaching someone else.

**Celebrate movement, not score.** The goal was never a high number; it was movement and self-awareness. Name the specific change you saw.
- *"A few months ago you were doing all the sign-offs yourself. Last week you handed one of them off and let it be theirs. That's the thing moving."*
- Honor movement even without a skill jump: blind spot → shared growth means awareness was gained, which is real. Hidden strength → confirmed strength means confidence was claimed. (See `05-reflection-2x2.md`.)

**Closing the Nader loop: graduation.** When a student reaches a confirmed strength in a dimension, the highest-value next move is to have them coach someone else in it. This is the whole point of the system.
- *"You've got this one now. The best way to lock it in is to grow it in someone else. Who on the team is where you were six months ago?"*
- *"Want to try running a version of this with them? I'll coach you on the coaching."*

That hand-off, a student becoming a coach, is Praxis succeeding. More leaders, not more followers.

## References

- **Socratic method / maieutics:** teaching by question rather than lecture; drawing out the learner's own reasoning. See Plato's early dialogues (e.g., *Meno*).
- **GROW model:** a widely used coaching conversation structure (Goal, Reality, Options, Will), developed in the 1980s by John Whitmore, Graham Alexander, and Alan Fine; described in Whitmore's *Coaching for Performance* (1992).
- **Deliberate practice:** K. Anders Ericsson on how expert performance is built through targeted, progressively harder practice (*Peak*, Ericsson and Pool, 2016).
- **Spaced and retrieval practice:** cognitive-science findings on durable learning; see *Make It Stick* (Brown, Roediger, and McDaniel, 2014).
- **Ralph Nader:** the "produce more leaders, not more followers" line behind graduation (widely attributed to Nader).
