# Praxis Coaching Expectations

*Praxis means putting ideas into use. You take a skill and practice it on real work until it becomes a natural habit.*

## Expectations of the Coach and Student

**We expect greatness of you.** That is the cornerstone of the coaching relationship.

**We are both experts.** You are the expert in your own work, context, and life. Your coach is an expert in development and in the process of drawing your answers out rather than handing them to you. At times the coach will offer a recommendation when you ask for one; more often they will ask the question that helps you find your own next step.

**Your coach will give assignments.** An assignment may include a specific action, what "done" looks like, and a rough timeframe. Your response is always one of three: (1) yes; (2) no; or (3) a counter-offer.

**Confidentiality and conflicts of interest.** Your coaching is held in confidence as far as it honestly can be. Where your coach also has a stake in how you are seen (for example, if they are also your manager), you name that up front and mark some goals as private, firewalled off from reviews, ratings, and decisions about your role. What is private stays private. If anything ever needs to cross that line, you hear it from the coach directly, at the time. Candor, not secrecy.

**Your coach wants your feedback.** For coaching to be worth the time, both people say openly what is and isn't working in the relationship. Raising a problem, a conflict, or a block in the coaching itself is your right and your responsibility.

**We both protect our meetings.** Sessions are scheduled in advance and both people guard them. If you have to cancel, give as much notice as you can, ideally a couple of days, and reschedule rather than drop it. The rhythm only works if we both treat it as real.

## The Role of the Student

You are a co-creator of the results, not a passive recipient. The coach brings the development process; you are the expert in your own work and role. Partnership and commitment are what make it work. As the student, you:

- Tell your coach about you, your work, and what you want out of this.
- Help set both public and private goals for your development.
- Stay open to feedback and to what a review or your own reflection turns up.
- Work with your coach to spot the real situations worth practicing on.
- Be honest about your priorities, and about your bandwidth.
- Invest the time, in sessions and on the job, to make the practice real.
- Be willing to try new things and take sensible risks.
- Focus on what you can actually do and practice inside your real work.
- Trade honest feedback with your coach about how the coaching is going.

## The Role of the Coach

The coach's job is to support the student's growth and success. Good coaches are honest, straightforward, caring, and appropriately pushy. They work from an appreciative stance: growing the person by leveraging strengths, expanding what is already working, and opening honest room for change and learning. As the coach, you:

- Work within the confidentiality and conflict-of-interest terms set with the student, and honor them strictly.
- Give the student the introductory materials up front (this document and the intro lecture) so they know how it works.
- Help the student turn a review, feedback, or their own reflection into a concrete next step.
- Name the difference between public and private goals, and keep private goals private.
- Present a few dimensions invitationally; let the student choose one, tweak it, or pass.
- Build a short, tailored path, and adjust the practice over time (keep, tweak, or swap) as circumstances change.
- Toward the end of a cycle, help the student name what moved and plan what's next, including graduating them to coach someone else.
- Stay available afterward, and check in occasionally to keep the gains alive.

## The Role of the Manager / Sponsor

Often the coach is also the manager; sometimes they are different people, or a separate sponsor supports the engagement. When there is a distinct manager or sponsor:

- The more you brief the coach on the context, the better the results.
- Work in partnership with the student and coach to shape the development needs and the outcomes worth aiming for.
- Give honest, complete context about the work and the environment.
- On goals, be direct about what matters most to the student's success.
- The coach will not share the content of coaching with you without the student's express permission, but they are your thinking partner in helping that person do their best work. You can offer input or feedback on progress at any time, and it is welcome.
- Your involvement is light and defined: usually goals alignment near the start and a wrap-up near the end, scheduled in advance with you, the student, and the coach.
