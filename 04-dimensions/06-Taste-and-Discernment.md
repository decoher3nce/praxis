# Taste & Discernment

**Definition.** The cultivated ability to know what's genuinely *good*: to set and hold a quality bar by sensibility and earned judgment, not just by checklist or metric.

**Why it matters here.** This is the difference between "passed the procedure" and "is actually right." A test organization runs on standards, but the highest standards live in the judgment of people who can tell good work from good-enough when no metric would catch the difference. A leader with taste raises the whole team's bar by what they notice and won't accept, and, crucially, by *teaching others to see it too*. Taste that can't be transmitted dies with the person; taste that's articulated becomes the org's standard.

**Behavioral indicators.** Can tell the difference between good and good-enough, and articulate *why*; holds a clear quality bar and protects it under time pressure; calibrates the bar to context (knows when "good enough" genuinely is); names the specific reason something is or isn't right rather than "I'll know it when I see it"; develops others' eye, not just their compliance.

**Lower vs. higher.**
- *Lower:* Judges quality by whether it meets the literal spec; can't say why something feels off. Either accepts mediocre work or demands perfection indiscriminately (no calibration). Holds standards as rules to enforce, not judgment to teach.
- *Higher:* Perceives quality finely and explains it in transferable terms. Holds a high bar where it matters and relaxes it where it doesn't. Protects the standard when the schedule pushes back. Grows the discernment of the people around them.

**Learning path.**
- *Read:* Paul Graham, "Taste for Makers" (2002): the argument that taste is cultivated judgment rather than personal whim, with concrete properties of good design.
- *Watch:* Ira Glass, "The Gap": a storytelling master on why your taste outruns your skill for years, and why sheer volume of work is how you close the gap.
- *Other:* sit with someone whose technical judgment you trust and have them walk you through *why* a piece of work is excellent or not.

**Activity sequence.**
1. **Frame & baseline.** Self-rate 1–5. Pick a recent deliverable and write *why* it was or wasn't good, in specific, transferable terms. *(low-stakes)*
2. **Input.** Engage the learning path. *(low-stakes)*
3. **Observe.** For a week, when you judge something "good" or "off," force yourself to name the specific reason. Log them. *(low-stakes)*
4. **Small experiment.** In one review, instead of giving a verdict, articulate the *criteria* of good for that work and coach someone to evaluate it themselves. *(low-stakes)*
5. **Visible stretch.** On one consequential piece of work, hold the quality bar against schedule pressure, and make your reasoning explicit enough that the team learns the standard, not just the ruling. *(visible stretch)*
6. **Reflect & integrate.** From memory: where could you articulate *why*, and where did you fall back on "I just know"? Did others' eye sharpen? Debrief with coach. *(retrieval)*
7. **Re-rate & decide.** Both rate; plot on the 2×2; decide continue / tweak / swap / graduate.

**Coach talking points (this dimension).**
- "What makes this good, in words someone else could use to judge the next one?"
- "Where are you holding too high a bar, and where too low? Is it calibrated to what actually matters?"
- "When the schedule pushes on quality, where's your line, and does the team know it?"
- "Whose eye on the team are you actively developing? How?"
- *(Blind-spot version, self-high/coach-low):* "You trust your taste. Can we test it on a few cases where I'd have called it differently, and see where our criteria diverge?"
