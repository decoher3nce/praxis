# Conflict & Collaboration

**Definition.** Turning disagreement and difference into better outcomes: engaging conflict productively rather than avoiding or escalating it, finding agreements that hold across competing interests, and knowing when to lead and when to defer.

**Why it matters here.** Hard technical calls generate real disagreement, and the org needs that disagreement *surfaced and resolved well*, not suppressed into false consensus or left to fester. A leader who navigates conflict productively gets the benefit of the dissent (better decisions) without the cost (damaged relationships). And collaboration includes its underrated half, *followership*: knowing when the better idea, the real expert, or the score should lead instead of you. A leader who can step back on purpose models distributed leadership and grows it in others.

**Behavioral indicators.** Engages disagreement directly and calmly instead of avoiding or steamrolling; separates positions from underlying interests; finds common ground and win-win structures; builds genuine teamwork across boundaries; defers to the better idea or the right expert without ego; repairs relationships after friction.

**Lower vs. higher.**
- *Lower:* Avoids conflict until it festers, or wins it by force. Argues positions, not interests. Treats disagreement as a threat to manage. Always needs to be the one leading; can't yield. Lets friction damage relationships.
- *Higher:* Steps into disagreement early and keeps it about the problem. Digs for the interest beneath the position. Designs outcomes both sides can own. Defers gracefully when someone else should lead. Repairs and strengthens relationships through the friction.

- *Read:* a short piece on interest-based negotiation (e.g., *Getting to Yes*), productive conflict, or followership. `[verify: describe topic if no confident specific source]`
- *Watch:* a talk on healthy conflict, difficult conversations, or collaboration. `[verify]`
- *Other:* observe someone skilled at defusing or resolving a tense disagreement; note exactly what they do.

**Activity sequence.**
1. **Frame & baseline.** Self-rate 1–5. Name a conflict you're currently avoiding and what each side actually *wants* beneath their position. *(low-stakes)*
2. **Input.** Engage the learning path; pick one frame (interests vs. positions, etc.). *(low-stakes)*
3. **Observe.** For a week, notice your default in disagreement: do you avoid, accommodate, or steamroll? Log the pattern. *(low-stakes)*
4. **Small experiment.** In one low-stakes disagreement, ask about the other person's underlying interest before arguing your position. *(low-stakes)*
5. **Visible stretch.** Step into one real conflict you'd normally avoid: surface it directly, work to the interests, and aim for an outcome both sides own. Or: deliberately defer to someone who should lead a call that's nominally yours. *(visible stretch)*
6. **Reflect & integrate.** From memory: where did engaging beat avoiding? Where did deferring serve better than leading? Debrief with coach. *(retrieval)*
7. **Re-rate & decide.** Both rate; plot on the 2×2; decide continue / tweak / swap / graduate.

**Coach talking points (this dimension).**
- "What conflict are you avoiding, and what's the avoidance costing?"
- "What does each side actually *want* underneath what they're arguing for?"
- "Where do you need to be the one leading, and where would deferring serve the mission better?"
- "After the last friction, did the relationship get weaker or stronger? What did you do to repair it?"
- *(Blind-spot version, self-high/coach-low):* "You see yourself as collaborative. Can we look at a recent disagreement from the other person's side and check how it felt to them?"
