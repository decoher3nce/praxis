# Custodianship & Legacy

**Definition.** Holding the craft, the standards, and the institution in trust for the people who come after: making decisions in light of the long-term health of the whole, and planting what you won't personally harvest.

**Why it matters here.** A test organization runs on hard-won, often tacit knowledge: the "feel" for a measurement, the memory of why a standard exists, the lesson paid for in a past failure. When senior people retire or move on, that knowledge can walk out the door, and a mission-assurance culture can't afford the re-learning. The custodian leads on a longer clock: they protect standards beyond their own tenure, transmit heritage knowledge deliberately, and grow the next generation of custodians. This is the Nader telos at its widest horizon: producing leaders who will outlast you, for an institution that will too.

**Behavioral indicators.** Protects standards and values that outlive their own tenure; deliberately transmits heritage and tacit knowledge before it's lost; makes decisions weighing the long-term health of the org, not just the current quarter; mentors the next custodians, not just the next performers; treats the institution as something held in trust, not used up.

**Lower vs. higher.**
- *Lower:* Optimizes for the current cycle; lets institutional memory erode. Hoards or neglects to transmit hard-won knowledge. Measures success only by what they personally deliver. Uses the institution rather than tending it.
- *Higher:* Stewards standards and knowledge for the long term. Actively captures and hands down the tacit "why." Weighs long-horizon health in decisions. Grows successors who will steward in turn. Leaves the craft and the institution stronger than they found it.

**Learning path.**
- *Read:* Peter Block, *Stewardship: Choosing Service Over Self-Interest*: stewardship as the willingness to be accountable for the well-being of the larger whole, operating in service rather than control.
- *Watch:* Roman Krznaric, "How to be a good ancestor" (TED 2020): trading short-term mindsets for long-term thinking, and our responsibility to the generations who inherit what we build.
- *Other:* interview a respected senior figure (or retiree) about the knowledge they fear is being lost, and capture it.

**Activity sequence.**
1. **Frame & baseline.** Self-rate 1–5. Name one piece of hard-won knowledge or one standard that would be lost if you left tomorrow. *(low-stakes)*
2. **Input.** Engage the learning path. *(low-stakes)*
3. **Observe.** For a week, notice where tacit knowledge lives only in someone's head, and where short-term pressure is eroding a long-term standard. *(low-stakes)*
4. **Small experiment.** Capture or transmit one piece of tacit knowledge: write it down, or teach it deliberately to someone who'll need it. *(low-stakes)*
5. **Visible stretch.** Take one real custodial act with a long horizon: protect a standard under pressure for the org's future good, or set up a deliberate transmission of heritage knowledge to a successor. *(visible stretch)*
6. **Reflect & integrate.** From memory: what did you preserve or hand down, and what's still at risk of being lost? Where did the long clock conflict with the short one? Debrief with coach. *(retrieval)*
7. **Re-rate & decide.** Both rate; plot on the 2×2; decide continue / tweak / swap / graduate.

**Coach talking points (this dimension).**
- "What hard-won knowledge would the org lose if you left tomorrow? Who else holds it?"
- "Which standards are you protecting for people who aren't here yet, and which are quietly eroding?"
- "Who are you growing to steward this after you, not just to perform, but to *hold* it?"
- "When the short clock and the long clock conflict, which one wins for you, and should it?"
- *(Blind-spot version, self-high/coach-low):* "You see yourself as a steward. Can we check whether the knowledge in your head is actually getting transmitted, or just assumed safe?"
